TacInno: Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models the Contact Dynamics Beyond Vision and Action

Weile Li, Yunfan Lou, Muqing Shi, Zihao Zhang, Zhenbiao Huang, Boxian Yao, Xiyu Zhu, Zhibo Pang

Published 2026-11-20 00:53 Asia/Shanghai Categories cs.RO, cs.CV Reading focus tactile_manipulation

Takeaway

TacInno targets the observation that adding tactile input to VLA models yields inconsistent gains, since much tactile change (such as gel deformation during gripper closure) is predictable from vision and executed actions. The method isolates contact dynamics that vision and actions cannot explain, termed tactile innovation, and uses it only as a training target: a tokenizer conditioned on visual observations and executed actions compresses future tactile change into a discrete code, and TacInno-VLA learns to predict that code without adding a deployment module. Reported results are average success rates of 87.5% across six simulation tasks and 70.5% across four real-world tasks, exceeding the strongest visuo-tactile baseline by 10.7 and 9.0 percentage points, with ablations supporting visual and action conditioning, multi-frame encoding, and auxiliary code prediction. Inspect how tactile innovation is computed and whether the gains hold when tactile change is largely visually predictable.

Original abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently been equipped with tactile sensing to perceive visually subtle contact events in contact-rich manipulation. However, adding tactile input yields inconsistent gains, and some visuo-tactile VLA models rely little on tactile input, because much of the tactile change, such as the gel deformation during gripper closure, is predictable from visual observations and executed actions. To address this problem, we introduce TacInno, which isolates the contact dynamics that vision and executed actions cannot explain, termed tactile innovation, and uses tactile innovation only as a training target. Specifically, the TacInno tokenizer, conditioned on visual observations and executed actions, compresses the future tactile change into a discrete code, and the policy, TacInno-VLA, learns to predict the code, adding no module for the code at deployment. TacInno-VLA achieves average success rates of 87.5% across six simulation tasks and 70.5% across four real-world tasks, outperforming the strongest visuo-tactile baseline by 10.7 and 9.0 percentage points, respectively. Ablation studies validate the contributions of visual and action conditioning, multi-frame encoding, and auxiliary code prediction. Together, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of tactile innovation as a supervision signal for contact-rich manipulation.

SADP: Subgoal-Aware Diffusion Policy for Long-Horizon Manipulation Learned From Foundation Model Generated Demonstrations

Site Hu, Takato Horii

Published 2026-10-09 Source IEEE Robotics and Automation LettersReading focus diffusion_flow_policy

Takeaway

SADP addresses long-horizon manipulation, where policies must coordinate intermediate subgoals and decide when to advance, while most imitation learning uses only task-level demonstrations and subgoal-level supervision is scarce. The framework uses foundation models to autonomously generate subgoal-annotated demonstrations, trains diffusion policies conditioned on both task-level and natural-language subgoal descriptions, and adds a lightweight auxiliary head predicting a continuation score that drives online subgoal switching and stage-level progress monitoring. Experiments in RLBench simulations and real-world evaluations on a UR5e robot show competitive task performance while exposing temporally aligned subgoal-level execution signals for progress monitoring. Inspect how generated subgoal annotations are validated and how the continuation score threshold affects switching behavior.

Original abstract

Long-horizon robot manipulation requires policies to coordinate multiple intermediate subgoals and determine when to advance between them. However, most imitation learning methods are trained solely on task-level demonstrations, without explicitly modeling the active subgoal or its execution progress. This limitation is further exacerbated by the scarcity of subgoal-level supervision in standard robot learning datasets, which makes explicit subgoal-conditioned control and online transition modeling difficult to learn. To address this issue, this paper proposes Subgoal-Aware Diffusion Policy (SADP), a framework that leverages foundation models to autonomously generate subgoal-annotated demonstrations and trains diffusion policies on these datasets. SADP structures policy execution around explicit natural-language subgoals by conditioning action generation on both task-level and subgoal-level descriptions. A lightweight auxiliary head further predicts a continuation score that drives online subgoal switching and supports stage-level progress monitoring. Experiments in RLBench simulations and real-world evaluations on a UR5e robot demonstrate that SADP maintains competitive task performance while exposing temporally aligned subgoal-level execution signals for progress monitoring. These results show that explicit subgoal progression can be incorporated into a diffusion policy without degrading task-level performance.

PhyDEX: A Benchmark for Physical Reasoning in Vision-Language-Action Models via Dynamic Extrinsic Dexterity

Hao Vo, Taisei Hanyu, Yuki Ikebe, Pham Tri Quang, Sreevenkata Anjani Tishita Godavarthi, Nghi D. Q. Bui, Daniel Sonntag, Chase Rainwater, Duy Minh Ho Nguyen, Anh Nguyen, Ngan Le

Inspect task construction and how physical-property inputs are supplied in the analysis.

Original abstract

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have advanced rapidly in language-conditioned robotic manipulation, yet existing benchmarks mainly evaluate gripper--object contact and largely ignore how objects interact with the environment through gravity, friction, inertia, and mass distribution. As a result, it remains unclear whether current policies truly understand physics or merely interpolate from their training data. We address this gap through the lens of dynamic extrinsic dexterity and introduce PhyDEX, a throwing benchmark in which a robot must infer an object's mass, center of mass, and friction to send it to a destination beyond its kinematic workspace. PhyDEX contains 35K trajectories (143 hours) across nine tasks and 100 scenes, covering uniform, irregular, and dynamic-mass objects with static, multiple, and moving destinations. All data are generated by a scalable, fully automatic pipeline that combines grasp generation, motion planning, and closed-loop throw optimization under randomized physical properties. We evaluate three representative VLAs, GR00T N1.7, DynamicVLA, and ForceVLA, and find that all achieve success rates below 3\%. Our analysis reveals two key bottlenecks: limited robustness of pretrained models, which causes most failures before the throw, and physical blindness, as providing ground-truth physical properties roughly doubles success. Experiments on a humanoid robot and in the real world show the same failure patterns. We hope PhyDEX will drive the development of physically grounded robot policies.

Contact-aided invariant extended Kalman filtering for robot state estimation

Ross Hartley, Maani Ghaffari, Ryan M. Eustice, Jessy W. Grizzle

Published 2020-01-16 Source The International Journal of Robotics ResearchReading focus robot_state_estimation

Takeaway

This work addresses legged robot pose and velocity estimation, where vision is sensitive to environmental and lighting conditions and existing alternatives fuse kinematic and contact data with IMU measurements. It develops a contact-aided invariant extended Kalman filter (InEKF) using Lie group theory and invariant observer design, combining contact-inertial dynamics with forward kinematic corrections to estimate pose, velocity, and current contact points, with error dynamics following a log-linear autonomous differential equation; it also covers IMU biases, contact addition and removal, and world-centric and robo-centric formulations. Inspect the observability and convergence arguments and the contact-schedule handling.

Original abstract

Legged robots require knowledge of pose and velocity in order to maintain stability and execute walking paths. Current solutions either rely on vision data, which is susceptible to environmental and lighting conditions, or fusion of kinematic and contact data with measurements from an inertial measurement unit (IMU). In this work, we develop a contact-aided invariant extended Kalman filter (InEKF) using the theory of Lie groups and invariant observer design. This filter combines contact-inertial dynamics with forward kinematic corrections to estimate pose and velocity along with all current contact points. We show that the error dynamics follows a log-linear autonomous differential equation with several important consequences: (a) the observable state variables can be rendered convergent with a domain of attraction that is independent of the system’s trajectory; (b) unlike the standard EKF, neither the linearized error dynamics nor the linearized observation model depend on the current state estimate, which (c) leads to improved convergence properties and (d) a local observability matrix that is consistent with the underlying nonlinear system. Furthermore, we demonstrate how to include IMU biases, add/remove contacts, and formulate both world-centric and robo-centric versions. We compare the convergence of the proposed InEKF with the commonly used quaternion-based extended Kalman filter (EKF) through both simulations and experiments on a Cassie-series bipedal robot. Filter accuracy is analyzed using motion capture, while a LiDAR mapping experiment provides a practical use case. Overall, the developed contact-aided InEKF provides better performance in comparison with the quaternion-based EKF as a result of exploiting symmetries present in system.
