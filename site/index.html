Trusting Imagined Futures: Auditing Imagined Rewards for VLA Post-Training

Zicheng Kong, Zhenbo Xu, Haoyan Zhang, Jinghan Yang, Zhenzhen Li, Yiwei Ru, Liuyu Xiang, WanTing Xu, Min Ren, Zhaofeng He

Original abstract

Video world models enable candidate actions to be evaluated through imagined outcomes, but high predicted rewards can favor trajectories with poor execution quality. We introduce Trusting Imagined Futures (TIF), an offline supervision selection method for vision-language-action (VLA) post-training that audits predicted rewards using execution feedback. A multidimensional reward model predicts a utility score for candidate ranking and five dimensions of execution quality. An auditor uses reward predictions and uncertainty to identify overestimation patterns learned from paired imagined videos and real executions. TIF accepts the candidate with the highest predicted utility when its advantage over the existing trajectory exceeds a margin and its audit risk passes a threshold; otherwise, it retains the existing supervision. The retained real trajectories then supervise behavior cloning. Across four LIBERO suites and three training seeds, TIF achieves a mean success rate of 76.25%, improving over training on the existing trajectories by 3.00 percentage points with the same trajectory count and training budget. Adding audit gating yields 1.47 percentage points with the reward model and advantage margin held fixed. The gains persist against advantage ranking and logistic regression risk selection at matched replacement counts. On recorded videos from three real robot tasks, TIF increases clean completion among selected trajectories from 58.33% to 70.00% across 60 candidate pools.

FloMo: 3D Scene Flow as a World Action Model Intermediate for Learning from Human Video

Jeremy Collins, Namra Patel, Ayush Agarwal, Shitij Govil, Animesh Garg

Published 2026-11-03 23:25 Asia/Shanghai Categories cs.CV, cs.AI Reading focus world_action_model

Original abstract

Pretrained video generation models distill internet-scale motion priors into robot policies, but full video is a redundant signal for action prediction. We argue that dense 3D motion is the medium through which video pretraining best transfers to robot actions, and that predicting motion rather than video affords better sample-efficiency for action prediction. FloMo is a 5B video-generation model fine-tuned to predict 3D scene-flow and action tokens. Rendering scene flow as RGB and encoding it into the same latent space as video enables a pretrained video generation backbone to efficiently adapt to predict 3D scene flow. Co-trained on action-free human video and action-labeled robot teleoperation data, our model benefits from human-video pretraining and generalizes to unseen objects, motion primitives, and tasks. Replacing motion prediction with a video prediction objective degrades out-of-distribution generalization, while adding a video prediction target alongside motion lowers policy success rather than improving it. When motion, video, and action are predicted jointly, action tokens attend far more to motion than to video, suggesting that predicting video wastes model capacity. Together, these results indicate that motion effectively mediates the transfer from video pretraining to action. More information can be found at flomo-wam.github.io.

Efficient Online Robotic Reinforcement Learning With Explicit Manifold Alignment

Wenkai Guo, Peizhi Tang, Yukun Bai, Ziming Yan, Haoyuan Deng, Guanxing Lu, Shijie Li, Ziwei Wang

Published 2026-08-28 Source IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters

Takeaway

REMA targets online reinforcement learning for contact-rich manipulation, where sparse binary rewards and hand-designed goal-proximity shaping fail to separate informative near-success failures from uninformative divergent trials. Given a small set of successful demonstrations, it builds a reference manifold by spectral compression of successful trajectories, treating low-frequency components as task-relevant motion patterns, and during online learning penalizes failed trajectories by normalized spectral distance to that manifold to suppress their influence on value estimates. Experiments report that REMA outperforms the evaluated baselines on the studied simulation and real-world tasks, and in human-in-the-loop real-robot RL it lowers intervention from 60.8% to 29.9% and raises autonomous success from 62.5% to 80.6%. Inspect how the spectral manifold and distance penalty are computed.

Original abstract

Online reinforcement learning (RL) enables robots to continuously improve through real-world interaction, yet achieving high success rates in contact-rich manipulation remains challenging due to sparse binary rewards. Conventional reward-shaping methods rely on hand-designed goal-proximity heuristics and seldom differentiate informative near-success failures from uninformative divergent trials, leading to inefficient exploration and potential reward hacking. By contrast, we propose Reinforcement Learning with Explicit Manifold Alignment (REMA) to refine credit assignment by penalizing uninformative deviations while preserving informative trials. Specifically, given a small set of successful demonstrations, we formulate that trajectory informativeness can be quantified based on the deviation from the manifold of successful behaviors, where exploring off-manifold state space introduces task-irrelevant noise into value estimates. We first construct a reference manifold via spectral compression of successful trajectories, where low-frequency components encode task-relevant motion patterns and serve as a proxy for informativeness. During online learning, the informativeness of failed trajectories is assessed via normalized spectral distance to the manifold, with distant trajectories receiving penalties to suppress their influence on value learning. Experiments show that REMA outperforms the evaluated baselines on the studied simulation and real-world tasks. In human-in-the-loop real-robot RL, it lowers intervention from 60.8% to 29.9% and improves autonomous success from 62.5% to 80.6%.
